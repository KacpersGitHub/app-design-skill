# UX psychology

Users don't decide logically. Defaults read as recommendations, the first number becomes the ruler, a gift creates a debt, building something makes it yours, and progress — even a head start — creates momentum. Use these to remove friction, never to trick: dark patterns destroy the trust everything else builds.

## 1. Smart defaults
- Prefill every field with the most common choice. Most users (often 70–90%) never change defaults; a default reads as "this is what most people pick".
- The user's job becomes "scan and adjust" instead of "fill from scratch".
- Fewer choices convert better (jam study: 24 options → 3% bought, 6 options → 30%).
- Make the button show the payoff: "See 12 results", not "Search".

## 2. Goal gradient — never start at zero
- People speed up as they near a goal. A loyalty card with 10 stamps and 2 pre-filled gets completed far more than an empty 8-stamp card.
- Count account creation (or the first answer) as step 1 and show progress already moving (~20%).
- Show profile/setup completeness that is never at 0.

## 3. Reciprocity — give before you ask
- Show a real, useful result before asking for sign-up (a score, a first plan, a preview of their data). Then: "Save your full report".
- Never blur results behind a sign-up wall — it feels like holding them hostage.
- Free trials, a generous free tier, and full-product use before payment all work the same way.

## 4. IKEA / endowment effect — let them build something
- People value what they made or chose. Let users pick a goal, name, theme, character or complete a first action before the account screen.
- The button says "Continue", not "Sign up". Leaving now means abandoning something theirs.
- Example: choose language, set daily goal, finish lesson one — then create an account.

## 5. Loss aversion — with guardrails
- Losses feel about twice as strong as equal gains. Framing an upgrade around what the user would really lose (their actual files, streak, history) beats listing abstract features.
- **Only real stakes and real deadlines.** No fake countdowns, no guilt-trip dismiss buttons ("No, I don't want to be healthy"). Always a plain, respectful way to decline.

## 6. Contrast / anchoring
- Every number is judged against the one seen just before it. Never show a cost alone.
- Show add-ons next to the main item ("$50 · just 2.6% of your order").
- On paywalls, show the higher option or the monthly price first so the recommended plan reads as the deal; show yearly as a per-month or per-week price.

## 7. Supporting principles
- **Peak-end rule:** people remember the best moment and the last moment. Invest in the success screen and the end of each session (a summary, a streak, a celebration).
- **Variable but honest rewards:** occasional delightful surprises (a new mascot reaction, a milestone animation) keep things fresh; never manipulative loot-box mechanics.
- **Commitment:** small first steps (pick a goal) make bigger ones (daily reminder) easier to accept.
- **Social proof:** real ratings and reviews near the decision point, not on a separate page.
- **Hick's law:** decision time grows with the number of options. Group, prioritize, and hide rare options behind "More".
